A mechanism can be real in a cell or animal model without being the mechanism responsible for a human supplement effect. Extract composition also varies strongly with plant part, cultivar, solvent and standardization.

1. Confirmed pharmacology

GABAergic system — strongest direct CNS evidence

Aqueous Withania somnifera root extract directly activated GABA-A receptors in electrophysiological experiments, with particularly strong activity at GABA-ρ1. GABA-ρ1 was reported to be about 27× more sensitive to the extract than conventional GABA-A receptors, and the extract could generate currents approaching those produced by GABA itself.

Importantly, withaferin A and withanolide A did not account for this activity in the tested system. The active constituent(s) remain unresolved.

Specific withanolides can modulate GABA-A

Separate experiments have reported positive GABA-A modulation by withanolide B and docosanyl ferulate, while withanolide A has shown inhibitory effects in some isolated-compound experiments.

Therefore: “ashwagandha = GABA agonist” is an oversimplification. The extract contains constituents with potentially opposing actions.

NF-κB / inflammatory signaling

Withaferin A has substantial experimental evidence for inhibition of inflammatory signaling, including IKKβ/NF-κB pathways, with downstream effects on inflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, COX-2 and iNOS.

Antioxidant / cytoprotective signaling

Ashwagandha extracts and individual constituents affect ROS, lipid peroxidation, antioxidant defenses, mitochondrial function and cytoprotective signaling in experimental models.

This is established as experimental pharmacology, but it should not automatically be assumed to explain acute subjective CNS effects.

HPA-axis / stress physiology

Human clinical studies of standardized root extracts support effects on stress-related outcomes, including cortisol. The exact molecular target producing these effects remains uncertain.

2. Plausible pharmacology

Serotonergic modulation

Evidence includes altered serotonin-related signaling in animal models, changes in serotonin receptor/transport-related measures, interactions of withanolide A with serotonin-system proteins, and older animal data showing altered 5-HT2 sensitivity after chronic ashwagandha.

Classification: plausible rather than confirmed human receptor pharmacology.

5-HT2 / 5-HT2A

Chronic ashwagandha increased functional 5-HT2 sensitivity in rats. Computational studies also predict interactions between some withanolides and 5-HT2A.

However, there is no convincing demonstration that KSM-66 directly activates human 5-HT2A receptors.

5-HT2 modulation: plausible. Direct 5-HT2A agonism: proposed.

Cholinergic / acetylcholinesterase

Withanolide A has shown acetylcholinesterase-inhibitory activity in computational and preclinical work. Human relevance remains uncertain.

NMDA / glutamatergic signaling

Withanolide A has been reported to reduce NMDA receptor expression in experimental epilepsy models. This is different from demonstrating direct NMDA antagonism.

Neurotrophic / BDNF signaling

Animal and cellular studies suggest effects on BDNF, neurogenesis, neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity.

HSP90 / proteostasis

Withaferin A and related withanolides interact with cellular stress and protein-homeostasis pathways, including HSP90-associated signaling. Relevance to ordinary oral supplementation remains uncertain.

3. Proposed / weaker pharmacology

Direct psychedelic 5-HT2A agonism

Not demonstrated. The hypothesis is supported only indirectly by computational binding, animal 5-HT2 sensitivity findings and a recent human hallucination case report.

There is no convincing receptor-functional experiment showing KSM-66 → 5-HT2A agonism → psychedelic signaling.

Dopamine modulation

Animal and cellular findings involve dopamine-related signaling, but evidence for clinically meaningful direct dopamine-receptor activity is weak.

Opioid-system modulation

Standardized Withania extracts can alter opioid receptor gene expression in experimental systems, but this does not establish opioid receptor agonism.

PPAR signaling

Experimental metabolic effects could involve PPAR pathways, but this is not established as a primary pharmacological target of ordinary oral ashwagandha.

KSM-66: the important distinction

KSM-66 is not simply “5% withaferin A.” Published KSM-66 material is standardized to roughly 5%+ total withanolides while withaferin A is reported below 0.1%.

Reported principal markers include withastramonolide A, withanoside IV, withanolide A and withanone.

Why the GABA finding matters

The whole-extract GABA activity is not explained by the best-known major withanolides tested individually. This leaves open the possibility that less-characterized constituents contribute substantially to acute CNS effects.

That is one of the more important unresolved questions in ashwagandha pharmacology.

Evidence note

This document summarizes the research discussed in the preceding analysis. It is a pharmacology overview, not a recommendation to use high doses. Mechanistic evidence from docking, cells or animals should not be treated as proof of human effects.

Key literature discussed: electrophysiological GABA-A/GABA-ρ1 work; ashwagandha 5-HT2 sensitivity experiments; withanolide receptor-interaction studies; withaferin A NF-κB/IKKβ work; clinical stress/HPA studies; and analytical KSM-66 composition studies.
