Cannabinoid Pharmacology Wiki

A structured reference covering the receptor pharmacology, mechanism of action, pharmacokinetics, subjective/physiological effects, and legal status overview of ten cannabinoids: THC, HHC, THCV, CBD, CBG, CBN, CBDP, CBC, CBT, and CBE.

Endocannabinoid system CB1 / CB2 pharmacology Phytocannabinoids & semi-synthetics Updated Aug 2026
Educational reference only. This page summarizes published pharmacology literature for general knowledge. It is not medical advice, not a dosing guide, and not an endorsement of use. Legal status varies enormously by country and even by U.S. state and changes frequently — verify current local law before acting on anything here. Several compounds discussed (HHC, THCV in some jurisdictions, CBDP, CBN in some contexts) are unregulated or newly regulated "novel psychoactive substances" with limited human safety data.

The Endocannabinoid System (context for everything below)

Nearly all cannabinoid pharmacology is described relative to two G-protein-coupled receptors, CB1 and CB2, plus a handful of secondary targets. Phytocannabinoids (plant-derived), semi-synthetic cannabinoids (chemically modified from plant precursors), and endocannabinoids (the body's own ligands, e.g. anandamide and 2-AG) all converge on this system to varying degrees.

CB1 receptor

Densely expressed in the CNS (basal ganglia, hippocampus, cerebellum, cortex). Primary driver of the psychoactive/intoxicating effects of THC-type cannabinoids. Also modulates pain, appetite, memory, and motor control.

CB2 receptor

Concentrated in immune tissue (spleen, tonsils, immune cells) with lower CNS expression. Associated with anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects; activation is not intoxicating.

Allosteric & secondary sites

TRPV1/TRPV2 ("vanilloid") channels, 5-HT1A serotonin receptors, PPAR-γ nuclear receptors, GPR55, and allosteric (non-orthosteric) sites on CB1 — engaged to varying degrees by CBD, CBG, CBDP and others.

Orthosteric vs. allosteric

Orthosteric ligands (THC, HHC, THCV at high dose) bind the same pocket as the natural endocannabinoid. Allosteric modulators (CBD, CBDP) bind a different pocket and change how the receptor responds to orthosteric ligands, without directly activating it.

How to read each entry

Every compound below follows the same template: identity and chemistry → receptor pharmacology → mechanism of action → pharmacokinetics → reported effects → legal/regulatory snapshot → research status. Data quality varies drastically — THC and CBD have decades of human trials; HHC and CBDP have only recent in vitro/rodent data; CBT and CBE have almost no dedicated pharmacology literature at all. Confidence is flagged with the limited data style tags where relevant.

Δ9-THC — Tetrahydrocannabinol

(−)-trans-Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol
Strongly psychoactive Phytocannabinoid
PubChem CID 16078
C₂₁H₃₀O₂MW 314.5 g/mol

Identity & source

The principal psychoactive constituent of Cannabis sativa/indica, biosynthesized from THC-acid (THCA) which decarboxylates to Δ9-THC with heat. The reference compound against which nearly every other cannabinoid in this wiki is benchmarked.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Partial agonist (orthosteric)High affinity (Ki ≈ 10 nM range); principal driver of intoxication
CB2Partial agonistComparable affinity to CB1; contributes to anti-inflammatory effects
TRPV1/TRPV2AgonistContributes to analgesic and anti-nociceptive effects
Opioid systemIndirect modulationInteraction implicated in reward and analgesia pathways
CB1 potency (relative)

Mechanism of action

As a CB1 partial agonist, THC dampens presynaptic neurotransmitter release (retrograde signaling), altering dopaminergic, GABAergic, and glutamatergic tone across reward, memory, and motor circuits. This underlies euphoria, altered time perception, short-term memory disruption, and appetite stimulation.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Onset: Inhaled ~seconds–minutes; oral ~30–120 minutes (first-pass metabolism to 11-hydroxy-THC, a more potent active metabolite)
  • Bioavailability: Inhaled 10–35%; oral 4–20% (highly variable)
  • Half-life: Highly lipophilic; distributes to fat, terminal elimination half-life can extend to days–weeks with chronic use
  • Metabolism: Hepatic CYP2C9/CYP3A4 to 11-OH-THC (active) then THC-COOH (inactive, the standard urine test analyte)

Reported effects

  • Euphoria, relaxation, altered sensory perception
  • Anxiogenic or anxiolytic depending on dose/context (biphasic, dose-dependent)
  • Analgesia, muscle relaxation, appetite stimulation
  • Short-term memory and psychomotor impairment; tachycardia
  • Tolerance and CB1 receptor downregulation with sustained heavy use
Legal snapshot: Schedule I/controlled in many jurisdictions federally in the U.S., while legal for medical and/or adult use under many individual state frameworks; legal medically or recreationally in a growing number of countries; status changes frequently — check current local law.

HHC — Hexahydrocannabinol

Hydrogenated (saturated) Δ9/Δ8-THC derivative
Psychoactive Semi-synthetic
PubChem CID 11067065 (9R)
C₂₁H₃₂O₂MW 316.5 g/mol

Identity & source

HHC is produced by hydrogenating THC (adding hydrogen across the C9–C10 double bond), a reaction first described in 1940. This creates a new stereocenter, producing two epimers: (9R)-HHC and (9S)-HHC. It occurs in trace natural amounts but the commercial supply is essentially all semi-synthetic, made from CBD extracted from hemp. It surged in the U.S. and European markets from roughly 2021–2024 as a purportedly "legal" THC alternative.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1 — (9R)-HHCPartial agonistBinding affinity comparable to Δ9-THC; ~5x lower potency than the synthetic cannabinoid JWH-018 in vitro
CB1 — (9S)-HHCPartial agonist (weak)~12x lower CB1 affinity, ~17x lower potency than the R-epimer
CB2Partial agonistAffinity broadly similar to CB1 for the R-epimer
μ-opioid receptorSome affinity reportedShared with THC; functional significance still under study
CB1 potency (relative)

Commercial HHC products are typically a roughly 1:1 racemic mixture of the two epimers, so overall potency sits below pure Δ9-THC, driven mainly by the R-epimer fraction.

Mechanism of action

Both epimers act as CB1 partial agonists analogous to THC, but recent signaling-bias studies indicate HHC does not simply recapitulate Δ9-THC pharmacology — it activates overlapping but non-identical downstream intracellular signaling pathways at CB1 ("biased agonism"), which may explain reported qualitative differences in subjective effects. Its saturated ring structure also makes it chemically more stable (resistant to oxidation into CBN-like degradation products) than THC.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Route: Primarily inhaled (vapes, flower spray) or oral (edibles, tinctures)
  • Metabolites: Cross-reactive with standard THC-COOH immunoassays, which can produce a false-positive "THC" drug test result despite no Δ9-THC being consumed
  • Stability: Greater shelf-life/heat stability than THC due to the saturated bond

Reported effects

  • Users and limited animal (tetrad assay) data describe THC-like effects — sedation, euphoria, analgesia, hypothermia — generally at lower potency than Δ9-THC
  • Products are unstandardized; potency and epimer ratio vary widely by manufacturer
Data caveat: Human clinical and toxicological data remain sparse. The WHO Expert Committee on Drug Dependence has reviewed HHC given its CB1 affinity and abuse-potential profile comparable to THC.
Legal snapshot: Regulatory status is unsettled and shifting fast. As of the mid-2020s, numerous European countries and a growing number of U.S. states have moved to explicitly ban or restrict HHC; other jurisdictions had no specific rule at time of its market emergence. Verify current status locally — this is one of the fastest-changing legal areas in this wiki.

THCV — Tetrahydrocannabivarin

Propyl homolog of Δ9-THC
Dose-dependent / mildly psychoactive Phytocannabinoid
PubChem CID 93147
C₁₉H₂₆O₂MW 286.4 g/mol

Identity & source

THCV is a "varin" — the propyl-chain (3-carbon) analog of THC's pentyl (5-carbon) side chain. Found in modest amounts in certain African and Asian cannabis landrace strains, and generally a minor constituent of most commercial cultivars.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Neutral antagonist at low dose; partial agonist at high doseDose- and concentration-dependent functional switch, a distinctive feature of THCV
CB2Partial agonistMay contribute to anti-inflammatory activity

Mechanism of action

At low/moderate doses THCV occupies CB1 without activating it, blocking endocannabinoids and other agonists from binding — functionally similar to a CB1 antagonist. This is the basis for research interest in appetite suppression and metabolic effects, the opposite of THC's orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) action. At sufficiently high doses THCV itself becomes a CB1 partial agonist and can produce mild, typically short-lived psychoactive effects.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Data largely extrapolated from THC-family kinetics; formal human PK studies are limited
  • Onset via inhalation reported as fast, with a shorter perceived duration than THC in anecdotal and small clinical reports

Reported effects

  • Appetite suppression (opposite direction from THC) — primary area of research/commercial interest
  • Reported clear-headed, energetic subjective quality at typical doses rather than sedation
  • Investigated in early clinical research for glycemic control and metabolic parameters in type 2 diabetes
  • At high doses: mild euphoria/psychoactivity, distinct from THC's intensity
Legal snapshot: Often treated similarly to THC due to structural similarity and its own (dose-dependent) psychoactivity, though some jurisdictions' hemp-derived cannabinoid rules don't explicitly name it. Regulatory clarity is inconsistent — confirm locally.

CBD — Cannabidiol

2-[(1R,6R)-3-methyl-6-(prop-1-en-2-yl)cyclohex-2-en-1-yl]-5-pentylbenzene-1,3-diol
Non-intoxicating Phytocannabinoid
PubChem CID 644019
C₂₁H₃₀O₂MW 314.5 g/mol

Identity & source

The second-most abundant cannabinoid in cannabis and typically the most abundant in industrial hemp. Structurally an isomer of THC (same molecular formula, different ring arrangement) but pharmacologically very different — it does not produce intoxication.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Negative allosteric modulator (NAM)Does not bind the orthosteric site; reduces CB1's affinity/efficacy for agonists like THC and anandamide
CB2Low-affinity partial agonist / inverse agonist (disputed)Weak direct activity; effects likely more indirect
5-HT1AAgonistImplicated in anxiolytic and antiemetic effects
TRPV1AgonistContributes to analgesic/anti-inflammatory profile
FAAHInhibits anandamide breakdown (indirect)Raises endogenous anandamide tone
PPAR-γAgonistLinked to neuroprotective/anti-inflammatory research

Mechanism of action

CBD's signature action is allosteric modulation of CB1 — it changes the receptor's shape/behavior via a distinct pocket (the same "ORG27569" allosteric site later shown to be shared by CBDP), reducing THC's intoxicating potency when co-administered without itself activating the receptor. This, combined with its broad polypharmacology (serotonergic, TRPV1, ion channel, and anticonvulsant sodium-channel effects), explains CBD's wide-ranging but comparatively modest per-target effects.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Oral bioavailability: Low (~6–19%) due to extensive first-pass metabolism; markedly increased when taken with fatty food
  • Half-life: Roughly 18–32 hours depending on route and formulation
  • Metabolism: Hepatic CYP3A4/CYP2C19 — clinically significant drug interaction potential (e.g., with anti-epileptics, blood thinners)

Reported effects

  • Anxiolytic effects reported in several clinical and preclinical studies, particularly for state/situational anxiety
  • FDA-approved pharmaceutical formulation (Epidiolex) for specific rare pediatric seizure disorders (Dravet, Lennox-Gastaut, tuberous sclerosis complex)
  • Anti-inflammatory and analgesic research across multiple models
  • Attenuates some THC-induced intoxication/anxiety when co-administered
  • Generally well tolerated; reported side effects include fatigue, diarrhea, and transaminase (liver enzyme) elevation at higher doses, especially combined with other hepatically-metabolized medications
Legal snapshot: Widely legal when derived from hemp with low THC content in many jurisdictions (e.g., under the U.S. 2018 Farm Bill framework and EU novel-food/cosmetics rules), though EU classification and individual national rules on CBD as a "novel food" or medicinal product vary. Prescription CBD (Epidiolex) is separately regulated as a medicine.

CBG — Cannabigerol

2-[(2E)-3,7-dimethylocta-2,6-dien-1-yl]-5-pentylbenzene-1,3-diol
Non-intoxicating Phytocannabinoid — "parent" cannabinoid
PubChem CID 5315659
C₂₁H₃₂O₂MW 316.5 g/mol

Identity & source

CBG is biosynthesized from cannabigerolic acid (CBGA), the universal precursor molecule that plant enzymes convert into THCA, CBDA, or CBCA — earning CBG the nickname "mother/stem cell cannabinoid." Because most of the CBGA pool is converted onward during plant maturation, mature cannabis typically contains only small amounts of CBG (~1% or less), though specialty cultivars are bred to retain higher levels.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Weak partial agonist / possible antagonist of THC at CB1Low intrinsic efficacy; not intoxicating at typical doses
CB2Partial agonistHigher relative affinity here than at CB1
α2-adrenergic receptorAgonistMay relate to reported sedative/analgesic effects
5-HT1AAntagonistOpposite direction from CBD at this receptor
TRPV1/TRPV2/TRPA1AgonistBroad TRP channel engagement, relevant to pain/inflammation research

Mechanism of action

CBG's pharmacology is broad and comparatively low-potency at any single target, similar in spirit to CBD but with a distinct receptor fingerprint — notably 5-HT1A antagonism (opposite of CBD's agonism there) and stronger TRP channel engagement. It does not meaningfully activate CB1 at typical concentrations, so it is non-intoxicating.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Human pharmacokinetic data are limited; most absorption/metabolism assumptions are extrapolated from other phytocannabinoids (low oral bioavailability, hepatic CYP metabolism)

Reported effects

  • Preclinical interest in anti-inflammatory, antibacterial (including against some resistant bacterial strains), and neuroprotective activity
  • Investigated in models of inflammatory bowel disease and glaucoma (intraocular pressure)
  • Consumer reports describe mild focus/alertness effects, though rigorous human behavioral data are sparse
Research status: Mostly preclinical (cell and animal) evidence; human clinical trials are limited relative to THC/CBD.
Legal snapshot: Generally treated the same as CBD in jurisdictions with hemp-cannabinoid carve-outs, since it is non-intoxicating and typically hemp-derived — but confirm locally, as some regulatory schemes only name specific cannabinoids explicitly.

CBN — Cannabinol

6,6,9-trimethyl-3-pentyl-6H-benzo[c]chromen-1-ol
Mildly psychoactive Degradation product
PubChem CID 2543
C₂₁H₂₆O₂MW 310.4 g/mol

Identity & source

CBN is not produced directly by the plant's enzymes in significant amounts; it primarily forms as an oxidative degradation product of THC over time (heat, light, and air exposure aromatize the THC ring). Consequently, aged/improperly stored cannabis is higher in CBN and correspondingly lower in THC. It's the first cannabinoid ever isolated, in the late 19th century, though its structure wasn't confirmed until the 1940s.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Weak partial agonistRoughly 10x lower CB1 affinity/potency than Δ9-THC
CB2Partial agonistComparatively higher relative affinity than at CB1
TRPA1/TRPV2AgonistPossible relevance to anti-inflammatory/analgesic effects
CB1 potency (relative)

Mechanism of action

CBN retains a modified version of THC's ring system after oxidation, preserving some CB1 affinity but with substantially reduced intrinsic efficacy, producing mild psychoactivity at typical concentrations found in aged cannabis products.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Less-studied kinetics than THC/CBD; presumed similarly lipophilic with hepatic CYP metabolism

Reported effects

  • Widely marketed for sedative/sleep-aid effects, though controlled human evidence specifically isolating CBN's sedative contribution (versus product terpene content or expectation effects) remains thin
  • Mild psychoactivity possible at sufficient concentration, generally described as weaker/duller than THC
  • Preclinical interest in appetite stimulation and anti-inflammatory activity
Research status: The popular "CBN = sleep" narrative outpaces the current rigorous clinical evidence base.
Legal snapshot: Regulatory treatment is inconsistent — some frameworks group it with other "hemp-derived cannabinoids," others flag it due to its (mild) CB1 activity. Confirm locally.

CBDP — Cannabidiphorol

Heptyl-chain homolog of CBD
Non-intoxicating (proposed) Very limited data
PubChem CID 49873141
C₂₃H₃₄O₂MW 342.5 g/mol

Identity & source

CBDP is one of the most recently characterized phytocannabinoids: researchers isolated and fully characterized cannabidiphorol (CBDP) and Δ9-tetrahydrocannabiphorol (Δ9-THCP) as new seven-carbon-chain ("heptyl") homologs of CBD and THC from the Italian medicinal cannabis variety FM2, published in 2019. It differs from CBD only in side-chain length — a 7-carbon ("phorol") chain in place of CBD's usual 5-carbon (pentyl) chain — and occurs only in trace amounts in plant material alongside its more publicized THC-analog counterpart, THCP.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Negative allosteric modulator (NAM)Recent structural work identified CBDP as a CB1 negative allosteric modulator engaging two distinct sites — the established ORG27569 allosteric pocket and a newly characterized intracellular site near transmembrane helices 1, 2, 6, 7 and helix 8
CB2Direct binding reportedIndirect endocannabinoid-system effects also proposed, similarly to CBD

Mechanism of action

Like CBD, CBDP does not appear to activate CB1 through the orthosteric (main) binding pocket; instead, mutagenesis and functional assays confirmed CBDP interacts with an intracellular allosteric site on CB1, changing how the receptor responds to true agonists rather than activating it directly. Its longer heptyl side chain, relative to CBD's pentyl chain, is thought to be responsible for markedly different potency at these allosteric sites compared to CBD itself, since its structural resemblance to known CB1R allosteric modulators motivated researchers to test it specifically as a negative allosteric modulator.

Pharmacokinetics

  • No dedicated human pharmacokinetic studies have been published; only very recent in vitro and rodent behavioral data exist

Reported effects

  • Early in vivo work in mice (tetrad assay, novel object recognition testing) is exploring whether co-administered CBDP can modulate THC-type effects, echoing CBD's role as a THC-moderating co-compound, but findings are preliminary
  • No established subjective effect profile in humans
Data caveat: This is an actively emerging research compound, not a characterized consumer cannabinoid. Almost everything known comes from 2019–2026 laboratory studies; treat any commercial "CBDP" product claims with significant skepticism given the near-total absence of human data.
Legal snapshot: Rarely named explicitly in existing cannabinoid statutes given how recently it was characterized; regulatory treatment is essentially undefined in most jurisdictions as of this writing.

CBC — Cannabichromene

2-methyl-2-(4-methylpent-3-enyl)-5-pentyl-2H-chromen-7-ol
Non-intoxicating Phytocannabinoid
PubChem CID 30219
C₂₁H₃₀O₂MW 314.5 g/mol

Identity & source

CBC is, alongside THC and CBD, one of the more abundant cannabinoids in many cannabis chemotypes, derived like the others from cannabigerolic acid (CBGA) via a distinct synthase enzyme (CBCA synthase). It shares CBD's basic isomeric formula with THC but adopts a distinct fused-ring ("chromene") structure.

Receptor pharmacology

TargetActionNotes
CB1Very low affinityEssentially non-intoxicating at physiologically relevant concentrations
CB2Weak partial agonistLow but measurable activity
TRPA1AgonistConsidered a primary mechanism for CBC's analgesic/anti-inflammatory research signal
TRPV3/TRPV4AgonistAdditional TRP channel engagement
FAAHWeak inhibitor (indirect)May modestly raise endocannabinoid tone

Mechanism of action

CBC's pharmacology is centered less on CB1/CB2 and more on the TRP channel family, particularly TRPA1, which is heavily implicated in pain and inflammatory signaling. This TRP-centric profile, rather than cannabinoid-receptor activity, is thought to underlie most of CBC's preclinical effects.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Limited dedicated human PK data; assumed to share the low oral bioavailability typical of lipophilic phytocannabinoids

Reported effects

  • Preclinical (rodent/cell) evidence for anti-inflammatory, antidepressant-like, and analgesic activity
  • Some interest in neurogenesis-related research (effects on neural progenitor cell viability in vitro)
  • Considered part of the broader "entourage effect" hypothesis when present alongside THC/CBD
Research status: Preclinical stage; minimal human clinical trial data specific to CBC in isolation.
Legal snapshot: Generally treated similarly to CBD/CBG in hemp-derived cannabinoid frameworks given its non-intoxicating profile, but explicit regulatory language naming CBC specifically is uncommon — confirm locally.

CBT — Cannabitriol

Hydroxylated THC derivative (multiple isomers, e.g. 8,9-, 10,10a- variants)
Minimal pharmacology data Minor phytocannabinoid
PubChem CID 156460
C₂₁H₃₀O₄MW 346.5 g/mol

Identity & source

Cannabitriol is a minor phytocannabinoid first identified decades ago, structurally a hydroxylated (extra –OH group) variant of the THC/CBN scaffold. Several isomeric forms have been described in the chemistry literature (differing in the position of the added hydroxyl and double bond). It occurs only in trace quantities in cannabis and has never achieved the research attention or commercial interest of the compounds above.

Receptor pharmacology

Dedicated CB1/CB2 binding-affinity studies specific to CBT are essentially absent from the modern pharmacology literature. Given its structural similarity to oxidized THC/CBN derivatives, some researchers speculate it may retain weak CB1 affinity, but this has not been rigorously established.

Mechanism of action

Not established. No receptor-binding or functional-activity studies with modern methodology (radioligand binding, cAMP/β-arrestin assays) appear to have been published specifically for CBT.

Pharmacokinetics

No human or animal pharmacokinetic data are available.

Reported effects

No controlled human data exist. It is generally presumed non-intoxicating or negligibly psychoactive based on trace natural abundance and lack of any reported recreational or clinical use pattern, but this is an inference rather than a tested finding.

Data caveat: CBT is one of the least-characterized cannabinoids in this wiki. Most available information is limited to early analytical/isolation chemistry rather than pharmacology. Treat any specific effect or potency claims about CBT with strong skepticism.
Legal snapshot: Essentially unaddressed in cannabinoid-specific regulation anywhere, owing to its obscurity and lack of any commercial market.

CBE — Cannabielsoin

Oxidative metabolite/degradation product in the CBD pathway
Minimal pharmacology data Minor / metabolic cannabinoid
PubChem CID 162113
C₂₁H₃₀O₃MW 330.5 g/mol

Identity & source

Cannabielsoin is a minor cannabinoid formed via oxidative transformation in the CBD degradation pathway (related to cannabidiol-hydroxyquinone intermediates), and has also been identified as a mammalian and fungal metabolic product formed during CBD breakdown, rather than a major direct plant biosynthetic product in its own right. It appears in only trace amounts in cannabis and aged CBD-containing preparations.

Receptor pharmacology

As with CBT, systematic modern CB1/CB2 receptor-binding characterization specific to CBE is essentially absent from the literature. It is chemically distant enough from the THC-type ring structure that meaningful CB1 orthosteric activity is not expected, but this has not been formally tested with contemporary assays.

Mechanism of action

Not established. No dedicated functional pharmacology studies appear in the modern literature.

Pharmacokinetics

No dedicated human PK data. Its main documented relevance is as a downstream oxidative metabolite/degradation marker of CBD rather than as an independently dosed compound.

Reported effects

No controlled human or robust preclinical behavioral data exist. Presumed non-intoxicating given its structural divergence from THC's active ring geometry, but again this is inference rather than a directly tested finding.

Data caveat: CBE is included here for completeness as one of the requested compounds, but essentially functions as an analytical/degradation marker in the literature rather than a characterized pharmacological agent. Any specific effect claims circulating commercially about CBE are not supported by published human data.
Legal snapshot: Not addressed in cannabinoid-specific regulation in any jurisdiction, owing to its obscurity and absence of any commercial market.

Sources & notes

THC, CBD, CBG, CBN, and CBC pharmacology reflects well-established literature accumulated over decades. HHC and CBDP sections draw on recent (2023–2026) peer-reviewed pharmacology studies, including in vitro CB1 receptor activation assays and structural/allosteric-site characterization work, cited inline where directly quoted. CBT and CBE sections are deliberately conservative given the near-absence of dedicated modern pharmacology studies for those two compounds — resist any commercial source that claims confident, specific effect or potency data for them.
