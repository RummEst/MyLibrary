Aripiprazole is a third-generation atypical antipsychotic belonging to the quinolinone class. It was discovered in 1988 by Otsuka Pharmaceutical (code OPC-14597) and first approved by the FDA in November 2002 for schizophrenia under the brand name Abilify. It is also approved for acute manic/mixed episodes of bipolar I disorder, adjunctive treatment of major depressive disorder, irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder, and Tourette’s disorder.

Its defining pharmacological feature is functionally selective partial agonism at dopamine D2 and D3 receptors combined with partial agonism at 5-HT1A and antagonism at 5-HT2A receptors. This profile allows it to stabilize dopaminergic tone — reducing activity where dopamine is excessive (e.g., mesolimbic pathway in psychosis) while supporting it where dopamine is deficient (e.g., mesocortical pathway). This mechanism underlies its efficacy against both positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia with a relatively favorable metabolic and extrapyramidal side-effect profile compared with many older antipsychotics.

Aripiprazole has high oral bioavailability (87%), is highly protein-bound (>99%), and possesses a long elimination half-life (~75 h for the parent compound and ~94 h for the active metabolite dehydroaripiprazole). Steady-state is typically reached within 14 days. Long-acting injectable formulations (Abilify Maintena, Aristada, Abilify Asimtufii) are available for maintenance therapy.

History and culture

Aripiprazole was synthesized and patented by Otsuka Pharmaceutical in Japan in the late 1980s (US patent 4,734,416 filed 1979; key antipsychotic patent US 5,006,528 filed 1989). It was first described in the scientific literature in the mid-1990s. Early research identified its unique partial agonist activity at D2 receptors, distinguishing it from both first-generation (full antagonists) and second-generation antipsychotics.

In 1999 Otsuka partnered with Bristol-Myers Squibb for co-development and commercialization. The FDA approved aripiprazole tablets for schizophrenia on 15 November 2002. Subsequent approvals expanded the label: bipolar mania (2004), adjunctive MDD (2007), pediatric autism irritability (2009), and Tourette’s disorder. Long-acting injectable formulations followed (Abilify Maintena 2013, Aristada 2015, Abilify Asimtufii 2023). A digital medicine version (Abilify MyCite) with an embedded sensor was approved in 2017.

Aripiprazole became one of the highest-selling antipsychotics globally, with peak annual sales exceeding US$7 billion before generic entry after patent expiry (US 2015). It is widely regarded as having introduced a paradigm shift toward dopamine system stabilizers rather than pure antagonists. In clinical practice it is frequently chosen for patients sensitive to metabolic side effects or requiring long-acting formulations.

Chemistry

Aripiprazole is an achiral quinolinone derivative chemically designated as 7-[4-[4-(2,3-dichlorophenyl)piperazin-1-yl]butoxy]-3,4-dihydro-1H-quinolin-2-one (also known as 7-[4-[4-(2,3-dichlorophenyl)-1-piperazinyl]butoxy]-3,4-dihydrocarbostyril). Molecular formula C23H27Cl2N3O2, molecular weight 448.39 g/mol. It contains a single hydrogen-bond donor and five acceptors; calculated LogP ≈ 4.55. Melting point range 138–150 °C.

Structurally it consists of a 3,4-dihydroquinolin-2(1H)-one (carbostyril) core linked via a butoxy chain to a 1-(2,3-dichlorophenyl)piperazine moiety. It is chemically related to other arylpiperazine compounds such as cariprazine, nefazodone, trazodone and etoperidone. Multiple crystalline polymorphs (at least twelve) have been characterized; anhydrous and monohydrate forms are used in pharmaceutical products.

Pharmacology

The primary mechanism of aripiprazole is believed to be a combination of partial agonism at dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT1A receptors together with antagonism at 5-HT2A receptors. At therapeutic doses it occupies a high proportion of striatal D2 receptors (often >80–90%) yet produces relatively low rates of extrapyramidal symptoms because of its partial agonist (rather than pure antagonist) activity. Intrinsic activity at D2 receptors is typically reported in the 25–60% range relative to dopamine, depending on the assay system; functional selectivity (biased agonism) has also been demonstrated.

Unique kinetic properties

Aripiprazole exhibits “flip-flop” pharmacokinetics in long-acting injectable formulations, where absorption rate from the injection depot becomes rate-limiting. Oral absorption is largely unaffected by food. Accumulation is predictable; steady-state concentrations of both parent and active metabolite are reached within approximately 14 days of once-daily dosing.

Dose modifications

Reduce dose by approximately 50% in known CYP2D6 poor metabolizers or when co-administered with strong CYP2D6 or CYP3A4 inhibitors. Further reduction may be required when both pathways are inhibited. Dose increase may be needed with strong CYP3A4 inducers. No routine adjustment required for mild–moderate renal or hepatic impairment; use caution in severe impairment.

Subjective effects

ℹ️
Effects listed below are compiled primarily from controlled clinical trials, product labeling, and post-marketing reports. Individual response varies widely with dose, indication, duration of treatment, concurrent medications, and personal factors.

Physical effects

Positive / desired

  • Reduced positive psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions)
  • Improved motor control / reduced tics (Tourette’s)
  • Lower risk of severe extrapyramidal symptoms relative to many antipsychotics

Neutral / variable

  • Mild sedation or mild activation (dose- and individual-dependent)
  • Minimal prolactin elevation in most patients
  • Generally weight-neutral to modest weight gain

Adverse / physical

  • Akathisia (restlessness) — most common EPS-related AE (often dose-related)
  • Nausea, vomiting, constipation, dyspepsia
  • Headache, dizziness, fatigue / somnolence
  • Insomnia or restlessness
  • Tremor, extrapyramidal disorder (less frequent than older agents)
  • Orthostatic hypotension (α1 antagonism)
  • Blurred vision
  • Weight increase (more pronounced in some pediatric populations)

Cognitive and emotional effects

Positive / therapeutic

  • Reduction of positive, negative and (to a lesser extent) cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia
  • Mood stabilization in bipolar mania
  • Adjunctive antidepressant effect in MDD
  • Decreased irritability and aggression in ASD

Neutral / variable

  • Alertness may be preserved or mildly reduced
  • Emotional blunting reported by some patients (as with other antipsychotics)

Adverse / cognitive / psychological

  • Anxiety, agitation, restlessness
  • Insomnia
  • Akathisia-related dysphoria
  • Rare: impulse-control problems (gambling, hypersexuality, binge eating) — black-box / warning language exists
  • Possible cognitive slowing at higher doses

Tolerance and discontinuation

Tolerance

Tolerance to the antipsychotic effect does not typically develop in the same manner as with substances of abuse. Some patients may experience diminishing response over years (common to many antipsychotics). Cross-tolerance with other dopamine D2 partial agonists (brexpiprazole, cariprazine) is expected on theoretical grounds.

Discontinuation / withdrawal

Abrupt cessation after prolonged use can produce rebound symptoms including insomnia, anxiety, agitation, and return of underlying psychotic or manic symptoms. Gradual taper is generally recommended, especially after long-term treatment. Because of the long half-life, plasma levels decline slowly, which may mitigate acute withdrawal intensity compared with shorter-acting agents.

Toxicity and harm potential

Acute toxicity

Overdose experience is relatively limited. Reported symptoms include somnolence, vomiting, tremor, akathisia, and, in severe cases, seizures or cardiovascular effects. Fatalities are rare; supportive care is the mainstay of management. The wide therapeutic index relative to many older antipsychotics is a clinical advantage.

Chronic toxicity

Long-term risks include metabolic changes (weight gain, dyslipidemia, hyperglycemia — generally milder than with olanzapine or quetiapine), tardive dyskinesia (incidence lower than with first-generation agents but not zero), and possible impulse-control disorders. Regular metabolic monitoring is recommended. Black-box warning exists for increased mortality in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis.

Abuse potential

Aripiprazole has negligible reinforcing properties and is not scheduled under controlled-substance legislation in major jurisdictions. Dependence liability is considered very low.

🚑
In an emergency: Contact local emergency services or poison control immediately. In the United States call 1-800-222-1222 (Poison Help) or 911.

Clinical implications

  • Long half-life supports once-daily dosing and provides a pharmacokinetic “buffer” against missed doses.
  • CYP2D6 poor metabolizers have approximately doubled exposure and longer half-life (~146 h); dose adjustment is recommended.
  • Steady-state monitoring (if needed) is best performed after ≥2 weeks of consistent dosing.

Legal status

Aripiprazole is a prescription-only medicine in essentially all jurisdictions. It is not classified as a controlled substance under the UN conventions or under the US Controlled Substances Act (Schedule N/A).
