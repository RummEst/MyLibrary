Risperidone is a benzisoxazole-derivative atypical (second-generation) antipsychotic developed by Janssen Pharmaceutica and approved by the FDA in 1993. It is used primarily to treat schizophrenia, bipolar mania, and irritability associated with autism spectrum disorder, and — at low doses — is used off-label for treatment-resistant depression augmentation, Tourette's, and severe behavioral disturbance in dementia (with a boxed warning against the latter use due to mortality risk in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis).

Its defining mechanism is combined high-affinity 5-HT₂A antagonism and D₂ antagonism, with 5-HT₂A affinity exceeding D₂ affinity — the prototypical "serotonin-dopamine antagonist" (SDA) profile that distinguishes second-generation from first-generation (purely D₂-blocking) antipsychotics. This ratio is thought to underlie its comparatively lower extrapyramidal symptom (EPS) burden at low-to-moderate doses relative to typical antipsychotics like haloperidol, though risperidone loses this advantage at higher doses because D₂ occupancy climbs steeply and the EPS-sparing margin narrows more than with other second-generation agents (e.g. quetiapine, clozapine).

Pharmacokinetically, risperidone is unusual in that its active moiety includes both itself and its major metabolite, 9-hydroxyrisperidone (paliperidone) — itself marketed separately as an antipsychotic. Because CYP2D6 activity varies genetically, parent-drug half-life ranges roughly sixfold between extensive and poor metabolizers, but the combined active-moiety half-life is comparatively stable (~20 h), which is why the drug is usually dosed once or twice daily despite the parent compound's short half-life in most people.

History and development

Risperidone was synthesized in the mid-1980s by a Janssen Pharmaceutica research team building on the company's earlier butyrophenone/benzisoxazole antipsychotic work, with the explicit design goal of combining potent 5-HT₂A blockade with D₂ blockade to reduce EPS relative to first-generation antipsychotics such as haloperidol (also a Janssen compound).

It received FDA approval for schizophrenia in December 1993, becoming one of the first widely adopted "second-generation" antipsychotics after clozapine (which carries agranulocytosis risk and requires blood monitoring). Approval was later extended to bipolar mania (2003) and to irritability in autism spectrum disorder in children (2006) — the first drug approved for that indication.

A long-acting injectable (LAI) microsphere formulation, Risperdal Consta, was approved in 2003, administered every two weeks, and later followed by once-monthly LAI formulations of its active metabolite paliperidone (Invega Sustenna). Risperidone went off-patent in 2008 and is now widely available as a low-cost generic, making it one of the most commonly prescribed antipsychotics globally.

Chemistry

Risperidone belongs to the benzisoxazole (specifically benzisoxazole-piperidine) structural class. Its structure links a 6-fluorobenzisoxazole moiety to a piperidine ring, which is in turn connected via an ethyl bridge to a tetrahydropyridopyrimidinone ring system. This hybrid scaffold — distinct from the phenothiazines and butyrophenones of first-generation antipsychotics — was purpose-built to achieve balanced serotonergic/dopaminergic antagonism.

Its major active metabolite, 9-hydroxyrisperidone (paliperidone), differs only by hydroxylation at the 9-position of the benzisoxazole-derived ring system and retains a nearly identical receptor-binding profile to the parent compound, which is why parent drug and metabolite are treated pharmacologically as a combined "active moiety."

Pharmacology

Risperidone acts as a high-affinity antagonist at 5-HT₂A and D₂ receptors, with 5-HT₂A affinity somewhat higher than D₂ affinity — the ratio classically used to define "atypicality" in the serotonin-dopamine antagonist model. It also has strong affinity for α1- and α2-adrenergic receptors and H1 histamine receptors, contributing to orthostatic hypotension, sedation, and weight gain, and negligible-to-low affinity for muscarinic (M1) receptors, which is why it produces relatively little anticholinergic burden compared to olanzapine or clozapine.

Unique kinetic properties

Risperidone is metabolized almost entirely to 9-hydroxyrisperidone (paliperidone) via CYP2D6, an enzyme with well-characterized genetic polymorphism. In CYP2D6 poor metabolizers (~5–10% of the population, more in some ethnic groups), parent-drug clearance is markedly reduced, prolonging parent half-life from ~3 h to ~20 h; conversely, because paliperidone itself is renally eliminated and pharmacologically near-equivalent to the parent, the combined active-moiety exposure is far less sensitive to CYP2D6 genotype than parent-drug levels alone would suggest — a key reason plasma-level interpretation for risperidone should always consider the parent + metabolite sum rather than parent alone.

Dose modifications

Lower starting doses and slower titration are recommended in hepatic or renal impairment, in the elderly (particularly dementia-related psychosis, which carries a boxed mortality warning and is not an approved indication), and when co-administered with strong CYP2D6 inhibitors (e.g. paroxetine, fluoxetine, bupropion) or CYP3A4 inducers/inhibitors, since these substantially shift active-moiety exposure. The EPS/prolactin side-effect burden rises disproportionately above ~6 mg/day because D₂ occupancy approaches the ~80% threshold associated with motor side effects, so incremental efficacy gains above that range are often outweighed by tolerability costs.

Effects

ℹ️
Compiled from clinical trial and post-marketing adverse-event data (prescribing information, meta-analyses of second-generation antipsychotics). Individual tolerability varies substantially, and risperidone's side-effect profile is notably dose-dependent — it behaves closer to a "typical" antipsychotic in EPS/prolactin burden at higher doses.

Physical effects

Therapeutic / desired

  • Reduction of positive psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions)
  • Reduction of manic symptoms in bipolar disorder
  • Reduced irritability/aggression (autism, off-label uses)

Neutral / variable

  • Sedation — dose- and individual-dependent
  • Appetite increase / weight gain — moderate relative to olanzapine/clozapine, more than aripiprazole/ziprasidone

Adverse / physical

  • Extrapyramidal symptoms (akathisia, dystonia, parkinsonism) — dose-dependent, more prominent above ~6 mg/day
  • Hyperprolactinemia — more pronounced than most other 2nd-gen antipsychotics due to relatively poor blood-brain barrier penetration relative to pituitary D₂ blockade; can cause galactorrhea, gynecomastia, menstrual irregularity, sexual dysfunction
  • Orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia (α1 blockade)
  • Metabolic effects: weight gain, dyslipidemia, increased diabetes risk
  • QTc prolongation (modest, dose-dependent)
  • Tardive dyskinesia (risk with long-term use, lower than first-generation agents but not zero)

Cognitive and emotional effects

Therapeutic

  • Reduced psychotic disorganization and paranoia
  • Mood stabilization in mania
  • Some improvement in negative symptoms relative to first-generation agents (modest effect size)

Neutral / variable

  • Emotional blunting / apathy — reported by some patients, dose-dependent

Adverse / cognitive / psychological

  • Sedation, subjective slowing
  • Akathisia-driven agitation/dysphoria (can be mistaken for anxiety or worsening psychiatric symptoms)
  • Rarely, depressive symptoms attributed to dopaminergic blockade ("neuroleptic dysphoria")

Tolerance and discontinuation

Tolerance

Therapeutic (antipsychotic) tolerance to risperidone's core effect is not typically described in the way it is for sedative or stimulant classes — efficacy is generally maintained with continued dosing, though some tolerance to sedative and orthostatic side effects develops over the first weeks of treatment. Cross-tolerance is not a major clinical consideration between antipsychotics in the way it is for e.g. opioids, since switching agents is usually done via cross-titration rather than direct substitution at fixed dose-equivalence.

Discontinuation / withdrawal

Abrupt discontinuation, particularly after prolonged use, can produce withdrawal/rebound phenomena including rebound psychosis (distinct from relapse of the underlying disorder), cholinergic rebound (nausea, diaphoresis, insomnia) if there was concurrent anticholinergic suppression, and withdrawal dyskinesias. Gradual tapering under medical supervision is standard practice, particularly for long-term users.

Toxicity and harm potential

Acute toxicity

Risperidone has a comparatively wide therapeutic index relative to older antipsychotics; acute overdose (case reports up to several hundred mg) has been survived, though fatalities have occurred, especially in combination with other CNS depressants. Overdose features include exaggerated pharmacological effects: sedation, hypotension, tachycardia, EPS, QTc prolongation, and rarely seizures or arrhythmia.

Chronic toxicity

Long-term risks include metabolic syndrome (weight gain, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance), tardive dyskinesia, osteoporosis/fracture risk from chronic hyperprolactinemia, and cardiovascular risk from cumulative metabolic burden. Elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis treated with risperidone (and antipsychotics generally) carry a boxed warning for increased mortality, largely cardiovascular and infectious (e.g. pneumonia).

Abuse potential

Risperidone has essentially no recreational abuse potential or reinforcing/euphoric effect; it is not a controlled substance. Misuse is rare and generally not associated with dependence in the classical sense, though physiological adaptation (discussed above under withdrawal) does occur with chronic use.

🚑
In an emergency: Suspected overdose or a severe reaction (e.g. signs of neuroleptic malignant syndrome — high fever, severe muscle rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability) is a medical emergency; contact local emergency services or a poison control center immediately rather than trying to manage it without professional care.

Clinical implications

  • Because the active moiety (parent + metabolite) has a more stable half-life than the parent alone, once- or twice-daily dosing works reasonably well despite the parent's short half-life in extensive metabolizers.
  • CYP2D6 genotype/phenotype and interacting drugs meaningfully shift the parent:metabolite ratio without necessarily changing total active-moiety exposure — a common source of confusion when interpreting risperidone plasma levels.
  • Renal impairment reduces paliperidone clearance disproportionately (since it's renally eliminated), warranting dose caution in renal disease independent of CYP2D6 status.

Legal status

Risperidone is a prescription-only medicine in essentially all jurisdictions and is not scheduled as a controlled substance anywhere, reflecting its lack of recreational abuse potential.
