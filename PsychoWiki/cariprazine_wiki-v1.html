Cariprazine (brand names Vraylar, Reagila) is a third-generation atypical antipsychotic of the piperazine carboxamide chemical class, developed by Gedeon Richter in Hungary and first approved by the FDA in 2015. It is approved for the treatment of schizophrenia, bipolar I disorder (manic, mixed, and depressive episodes), and as an adjunct to antidepressants in major depressive disorder (MDD).

What distinguishes cariprazine from all preceding antipsychotics is its exceptionally high and preferential affinity for the dopamine D3 receptor (Ki = 0.085 nM), approximately six- to ten-fold greater than for the D2 receptor. It acts as a partial agonist at both D3 and D2, a partial agonist at 5-HT1A, and an antagonist at 5-HT2A and 5-HT2B. It has no affinity for muscarinic receptors and low affinity for histamine H1 and adrenergic receptors, conferring a comparatively benign metabolic and sedation profile.

Its primary active metabolite, didesmethylcariprazine (DDCAR), has a half-life of 1–3 weeks, making cariprazine pharmacokinetically unusual: full therapeutic and side-effect profiles may not emerge for weeks after initiation or dose changes, and the drug remains detectably active for months after the last dose.

History and culture

Cariprazine emerged from a Hungarian drug discovery program at Gedeon Richter in the early 2000s, as researchers sought a dopamine partial agonist with pronounced D3 selectivity — a receptor implicated in cognitive function, reward, and the negative symptoms of schizophrenia that existing antipsychotics failed to address.

It entered clinical trials under the code name RGH-188, and received FDA approval in September 2015 under the brand name Vraylar. European approval as Reagila followed in 2017. Its approval for bipolar depression (2019) and MDD augmentation (2023) expanded its clinical footprint significantly. As of 2025, Vraylar is among the highest-grossing branded psychiatric drugs in the United States, with AbbVie (following its Allergan acquisition) holding U.S. commercialization rights.

Community interest in cariprazine among self-experimenters has grown modestly, driven by its unique receptor profile and anecdotal reports of pro-social, pro-motivational, and cognitive-enhancing effects distinct from the dulling profile of traditional antipsychotics. Reports on forums describe occasional off-label use for depression, social anxiety, and ADHD-like symptoms, reflecting D3/D1 pathway involvement in these conditions.

Chemistry

Cariprazine is chemically designated as trans-N-[4-[2-[4-(2,3-dichlorophenyl)piperazin-1-yl]ethyl]cyclohexyl]-N′,N′-dimethylurea dihydrochloride. It belongs to the piperazine carboxamide class, containing:

  • A 2,3-dichlorophenylpiperazine moiety — the pharmacophore responsible for dopamine receptor binding, shared with several other psychoactive agents
  • An ethyl bridge linking to a trans-cyclohexyl group
  • A disubstituted urea terminus (N,N-dimethylcarbamoylamino group)

The 2,3-dichloro substitution on the phenyl ring is critical for D3/D2 binding selectivity and affinity. Structural analogs with different halogen patterns (e.g., 2,4-dichloro) show markedly reduced D3 affinity.

Pharmacology

Cariprazine is a dopamine system stabilizer — a partial agonist with biased signalling properties. Its state-dependent activity means it functions as a functional antagonist when endogenous dopamine is high (suppressing psychosis) and as a functional agonist when dopamine is low (improving motivation and negative symptoms). This represents a qualitative advance over the binary blocking approach of older antipsychotics.

Unique kinetic properties

Cariprazine exhibits an unusual biphasic dissociation profile at the D3 receptor — it dissociates rapidly from one receptor conformation but slowly from another. This may enable rapid responsiveness to fluctuating dopamine levels, contributing to its effectiveness in both psychosis (high-DA states) and depression (low-DA states). At D2, it shows slow, monophasic dissociation, providing sustained buffering without the hit-and-run binding of clozapine or the tight grip of haloperidol.

ℹ️
Titration note: Due to accumulation of DDCAR (t½ 1–3 weeks), plasma levels do not reach steady state for 4–8 weeks. Adverse effects may first appear weeks after initiation or dose increase. Dose changes should not be made more frequently than every 1–2 weeks.

Dose modifications

  • Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, clarithromycin, ritonavir): reduce cariprazine dose by 50%
  • CYP3A4 inducers (rifampicin, carbamazepine): avoid concomitant use; efficacy will be severely reduced
  • Hepatic impairment: use with caution in mild-moderate; avoid in severe
  • Renal impairment: no dose adjustment required for mild-severe
  • Elderly: start at lowest dose; avoid in elderly patients with dementia-related psychosis (black box warning)

Subjective effects

📋
The following effects are based on community reports and published clinical trial data. Effects accumulate over weeks and vary significantly by dose, individual, diagnosis, and concomitant medications. Intensity ratings reflect typical clinical doses (1.5–6 mg/day). Cariprazine is not a classic psychoactive — it does not produce acute intoxication. Effects are subtle and cumulative.

Physical effects

Positive (desired)

  • Reduced psychomotor agitation moderate
  • Improved sleep onset and quality mild via H1/5-HT2A
  • Decreased restlessness in manic states moderate
  • Reduced tremor and motor symptoms vs. FGAs mild
  • Metabolic neutrality — minimal weight gain vs. olanzapine/clozapine
  • Normal prolactin levels — no galactorrhoea/sexual dysfunction from hyperprolactinaemia

Neutral / variable

  • Mild sedation mild–moderate more common at initiation
  • Mild nausea mild usually first 1–2 weeks
  • Slight dizziness on standing mild
  • Mild fatigue / asthenia mild
  • Constipation (occasional)
  • Mild headache (early treatment)

Adverse physical

  • Akathisia — inner restlessness, compulsion to move common (7–16%) peak onset weeks 1–3; typically mild/moderate; dose-dependent
  • Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) — tremor, rigidity uncommon (~5%)
  • Insomnia moderate particularly at higher doses
  • Vomiting uncommon
  • CPK (creatine kinase) elevation rare; monitor if muscle pain occurs
  • Tardive dyskinesia very rare lower risk than FGAs due to partial agonism
  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) rare but serious
  • QTc prolongation minimal at recommended doses; not clinically significant at 3× max dose in trials
  • New-onset diabetes mellitus reported; mechanism unclear given low 5-HT2C/H1 affinity
  • Hypertension mild–occasional

Cognitive and emotional effects

Positive (therapeutic)

  • Reduction in hallucinations and delusions strong (positive symptoms)
  • Improvement in negative symptoms of schizophrenia — flat affect, anhedonia, avolition moderate–strong unique advantage vs. most antipsychotics
  • Pro-motivational effects reported by users; D3/5-HT1A mechanism
  • Improved social engagement and reduced social withdrawal
  • Antidepressant effects in bipolar depression and MDD moderate
  • Mood stabilisation in mania/hypomania
  • Pro-cognitive effects — improved attention, working memory mild more pronounced than most antipsychotics
  • Reduced anxiety (secondary to 5-HT1A partial agonism)
  • Emotional blunting is less severe than with D2 antagonists user reports

Neutral / variable cognitive

  • Mildly altered time perception (early treatment)
  • Reduced impulsivity D3 modulation of impulse control circuits
  • Hypersexuality rare case reports; possibly D3-mediated reward sensitisation
  • Vivid dreams (occasional)
  • Emotional flattening at higher doses mild

Adverse cognitive / psychological

  • Akathisia (subjective) — psychological restlessness, dysphoria uncommon but distressing
  • Sedation / cognitive dulling at high doses mild
  • Impulse control symptoms rare; gambling, compulsive eating
  • Delayed-onset adverse effects may appear up to 10+ weeks after initiation due to DDCAR accumulation
  • Suicidal ideation black box warning for MDD augmentation especially in under-25s

Tolerance and discontinuation

Tolerance

Clinical tolerance to the therapeutic effects of cariprazine has not been formally characterised. D2/D3 receptor upregulation (supersensitivity) may develop with chronic use, as seen with other antipsychotics — this is the pharmacological basis of rebound psychosis and tardive effects upon discontinuation. Some users report reduced akathisia over weeks, suggesting partial tolerance to this adverse effect.

Discontinuation / withdrawal

Due to the extraordinarily long half-life of DDCAR (1–3 weeks), abrupt discontinuation produces a very gradual pharmacological taper automatically. Acute discontinuation syndrome (cholinergic rebound, nausea, vomiting, insomnia) is less prominent than with high-anticholinergic antipsychotics. However:

  • D2/D3 receptor supersensitivity from chronic blockade/partial agonism → rebound psychosis or mania weeks after stopping
  • Return of underlying illness symptoms as therapeutic level falls
  • Residual akathisia may persist briefly post-discontinuation
  • The pharmacokinetic taper is not a substitute for clinical tapering strategy — abrupt cessation is not recommended
⚠️
Never discontinue cariprazine abruptly without medical supervision. The apparent "slow washout" due to DDCAR half-life can mask rapid clinical relapse once levels finally fall below therapeutic threshold. Tapering under psychiatric guidance is essential.

Toxicity and harm potential

Acute toxicity

Limited overdose data exist. No deaths from cariprazine overdose alone have been reported in the literature. At supratherapeutic doses, the primary risks are extension of adverse effects:

  • Severe akathisia and EPS
  • Sedation and hypotension
  • QTc prolongation — not clinically significant at 3× max dose in controlled trials, but risk may increase with higher doses or in combination with other QT-prolonging agents
  • No respiratory depression (not an opioid or GABAergic)
  • No known lethal dose in humans from compound alone

Chronic toxicity

  • Tardive dyskinesia — lower risk than FGAs but not zero; monitor for abnormal movements
  • New-onset diabetes mellitus — observed in trials despite lack of expected mechanism (low H1/5-HT2C); mechanism under investigation
  • Weight gain — mean +1.58 kg over clinical trials; substantially lower than olanzapine (+3–5 kg)
  • CPK elevations — occasional; rule out rhabdomyolysis if muscle pain present
  • Liver enzyme changes — generally not clinically significant

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS)

All antipsychotics carry a risk of NMS — a rare, life-threatening syndrome characterised by hyperthermia, muscle rigidity, altered consciousness, and autonomic instability. If suspected, stop cariprazine immediately and seek emergency care.

Abuse potential

Cariprazine is not a controlled substance and has no established recreational abuse potential. Partial D2/D3 agonism actually produces less dopaminergic stimulation than full agonists, and the lack of euphoric effects makes misuse unlikely. It is not physiologically addictive in the classical sense, though psychological dependence on therapeutic effects is possible.

Clinical implications of long half-life

  • Dose changes take 4–8 weeks to reach new steady state — do not escalate rapidly
  • Adverse effects may first appear weeks after initiation (delayed-onset akathisia documented at 10+ weeks)
  • After a single 1 mg dose, DDCAR remains detectable for up to 8 weeks
  • Drug interactions affecting CYP3A4 alter exposure for an extended period
  • A missed dose has minimal short-term consequence — once-daily dosing is pharmacokinetically justified

Legal status

Cariprazine is a prescription-only (Schedule ℞) medication in all jurisdictions where it is available. It is not a controlled substance in the United States, the European Union, or elsewhere. It has no scheduling under the UN drug conventions. Illicit production or sale without a prescription would violate pharmaceutical regulations rather than drug control laws specifically.
